# Social Security 2027: 4 Changes Every Retiree Should Check Before January

## hook

In January, nearly 72 million Americans get a 3.6% raise from Social Security. But the raise is only one of four changes for 2027.

One of them decides how much you can earn at work before your benefits are held back. Another raises the tax bill for higher earners. And one changes how much work it takes to earn a Social Security credit.

By the end of this video, you will know which of the four apply to you, and what to check before January.

## intro

## change-1

Change number one. The raise.

On October 14, Social Security announced a 3.6% increase for 2027. On average, retirement benefits go up by $75 a month.

But $75 is an average. Your raise is 3.6% of your own benefit. If you receive $2,000 a month today, that is $72 more.

And that is before deductions. Your notice lists any deductions, and for people on Medicare, the 2027 premium amount arrives in the same place.

So the number that matters is not 3.6%. It is the dollar amount on your own notice.

Here is what to check. Social Security sends every beneficiary a one-page notice, with the exact dates and dollar amounts of the new benefit.

People who enrolled in paperless notices by November 18 see it online first, in late November. That is up to three weeks before the paper copy.

Everyone else gets the notice by mail in December. When it arrives, read the dollar amount, not the percentage.

## change-2

Change number two. Working while you collect.

If you take Social Security before your full retirement age and keep working, there is a limit on what you can earn. Go over it, and Social Security holds back part of your benefits. The rule is called the retirement earnings test. In 2027, the limit goes up.

First, what is full retirement age? It depends on the year you were born. If you were born in 1959, it is 66 and 10 months. If you were born in 1960 or later, it is 67.

There are two limits, and the one you get depends on where you are in that timeline.

The first limit is for people who are under full retirement age for the whole year. In 2026, that limit is $24,480. In 2027, it rises to $25,560. That is $2,130 a month.

Above that line, Social Security withholds $1 in benefits for every $2 you earn over the limit.

Here is an example. You are 63, you collect benefits, and you earn $35,560 at your job in 2027. That is $10,000 over the limit. Half of it, $5,000, is held back from your benefits.

The second limit is for the year you reach full retirement age. If you were born in 1960, that year is 2027.

For that year, the limit is much higher. It rises from $65,160 to $67,920. And the rate is gentler. Social Security withholds $1 for every $3 over the limit.

Another example. You turn 67 in September 2027. From January to August, you earn $73,920. That is $6,000 over the limit, so $2,000 is held back.

Two details matter here. Only the months before you reach full retirement age count. And from the month you reach it, there is no limit at all. You can earn any amount, and nothing is held back.

Now, what counts as earnings? The regulation is specific. It is your wages from a job, plus your net earnings from self-employment. The test looks at money you earn by working.

There is also a rule for your first year. The regulation calls it a grace year. In that year, Social Security can pay your benefit for any month in which your wages stay under the monthly limit and you are not working in your own business. What you earned earlier in the year does not matter for that month.

So if you retire in the middle of 2027 after a strong first half, the figure to watch is the monthly one. $2,130.

And here is the part many people miss. Money held back by the earnings test is not simply gone. Under the regulations, the months in which benefits were withheld are not counted when Social Security works out your reduction for claiming early. When you reach full retirement age, the adjustment is automatic, and your benefit goes up from that month.

One more thing. If your spouse or your child receives benefits on your record, your extra earnings can reduce their benefits too.

So what should you check before January? First, find out which limit applies to you in 2027. $25,560, $67,920, or none. Second, estimate what you will earn next year. Third, if that estimate has changed, tell Social Security. The agency withholds benefits based on your estimated earnings, starting from the beginning of the year. A good estimate avoids surprises.

## change-3

Change number three. The tax cap.

This one affects people who are still working and earning a high income.

Social Security tax is not charged on every dollar you earn. It stops at a ceiling. The official name is the contribution and benefit base.

In 2026, that ceiling is $184,500. In 2027, it rises to $192,300. That is $7,800 more of your pay that can be taxed.

The rate, according to the IRS, is 6.2% for the employee and 6.2% for the employer.

So let us do the math. At $184,500, the most an employee pays is $11,439. At $192,300, it is $11,922.60. That is $483.60 more in 2027, for someone who earns at or above the new ceiling.

Two quick examples. If you earn $150,000, nothing changes. You were already under the ceiling, and you still are. If you earn $250,000, you pay the full $483.60 more.

There is a second side to this ceiling. The same number is also the most earnings Social Security counts when it works out your benefit. So a higher ceiling means high earners pay more in, and also have more earnings counted toward their future benefit.

Here is what to check. If you have one employer, your payroll handles it. The Social Security line on your pay stub should stop once you pass $192,300 for the year.

If you have two or more employers, look closer. The IRS says that with more than one employer, the total withheld can end up above the maximum. In that case, you may be able to claim the excess as a credit on your income tax return. So keep every W-2.

## change-4

Change number four. The work credit.

To receive retirement benefits at all, you need enough work credits. Social Security calls them quarters of coverage. You earn them by working and paying into the system.

In 2026, you need $1,890 in earnings for one credit. In 2027, it takes $1,970.

You can earn at most four credits in a year, no matter how much you make. So for a full year of credits in 2027, you need $7,880 in earnings. That is $320 more than this year.

How many do you need? For anyone reaching 62 now, the regulations work out to 40 credits. At four a year, that is ten years of work.

Who should care? Most people who worked full time for decades passed 40 credits long ago. For them, this change means nothing.

But it matters for some people. If you spent years out of paid work, raising children or caring for a parent. If you worked in a job that did not pay into Social Security. If you came to the United States later in life.

Here is how the math works. With 36 credits, you need four more. That is one year with at least $7,880 in earnings. With 30 credits, you need ten more. That is two and a half years.

And the credit is based on what you earn in the year, not on which months you worked. Earn $7,880 in 2027, even in a few months, and you get all four credits.

Self-employment counts too, as long as the income is reported.

Here is what to check. Sign in to your my Social Security account and open your earnings record. Count your credits. Then look at each year for mistakes. A missing year of earnings can mean missing credits.

## who

So which of the four apply to you? It depends on your situation. Here are four common ones.

You are retired, you collect Social Security, and you do not work. Only the first change touches you. Read your notice when it arrives.

You collect Social Security and you still work, and you have not reached full retirement age. Changes one and two apply. The raise lifts your benefit, and the earnings limit decides how much of it you keep.

You are still working, you have not claimed yet, and you earn a high salary. Change three applies. If your pay is above $184,500, your Social Security tax goes up next year.

You have a short work history in the United States. Change four applies. Check how many credits you have, and how many you still need.

And if you have already reached full retirement age and you keep working, here is the good news. The earnings limit no longer applies to you at all.

## why

Before the checklist, one question. Why do these numbers move every year?

The raise follows prices. The other three follow wages. Each year, Social Security publishes a national average wage index. For 2025, it is $72,800. The earnings limits, the tax ceiling and the credit amount are all calculated from that figure.

So when average wages rise, all three rise with them. It happens almost every year, and it is published in the Federal Register before November.

That is why this is worth ten minutes every autumn. The rules stay the same. The numbers do not.

## recap

Let us put the four together. Before January, check these.

One. Your notice. Read the new dollar amount, after deductions. It arrives online in late November, or by mail in December.

Two. If you work and collect before full retirement age, know your limit. $25,560, or $67,920 in the year you reach full retirement age. Then update your earnings estimate with Social Security.

Three. If you earn more than $184,500, expect up to $483.60 more in Social Security tax next year. With two employers, keep your W-2 forms.

Four. If you are short of 40 credits, plan for $7,880 in earnings in 2027, and check your earnings record for missing years.

Not all four will apply to you. Pick the ones that do, and put a date on your calendar.
